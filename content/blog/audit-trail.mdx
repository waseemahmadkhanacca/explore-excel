The question always arrives at the worst time. Someone asks where a number came from, and the honest answer is that you cannot reconstruct it.

I ask this question professionally. What follows is what makes the answer easy, written from the perspective of the person doing the asking.

## Why it matters even without an auditor

You are the main beneficiary of your own audit trail. Six months is long enough to forget your own reasoning entirely, and the workbook is the only record.

The cost of building the trail is perhaps twenty minutes. The cost of reconstructing one after the fact is measured in days, and sometimes it cannot be done at all.

## Keep the source untouched

The raw export goes on its own sheet and nothing is done to it. No sorting, no deleting, no fixing.

Cleaning happens on a separate sheet that references it, or in Power Query. That way the original remains available for comparison, and "does the cleaned data still match the export" becomes a question you can answer rather than assert.

The commonest failure I see is a single sheet that has been cleaned in place over several months. There is no way to verify anything, because the thing it should be verified against no longer exists.

## Every assumption in its own cell

A number typed inside a formula is invisible. It cannot be found by searching, it cannot be reviewed, and it cannot be changed without editing formulas.

Put assumptions in a labelled block — rate, threshold, date, whatever — and reference them. An assumptions sheet is the fastest thing to review and the first thing anyone competent will look at.

Add a note beside each one saying where the figure came from. "Per Finance email 14 March" takes five seconds and answers a question that would otherwise take an hour.

## Make the calculation visible

A long formula doing five things at once is hard to check and harder to explain.

```
=ROUND(IFERROR(INDEX(Rates!C:C, MATCH(A2, Rates!A:A, 0)), 0) * B2 * (1 + $D$2), 2)
```

Broken into three columns — rate, base amount, adjusted — it becomes reviewable, and when a number is wrong you can see immediately which step failed.

Extra columns cost nothing. Reviewability is worth a great deal.

## Date and version the file

`Reconciliation.xlsx` tells nobody anything. `Reconciliation-2026-07-final.xlsx` tells them when, and `final-v2-updated` tells them the process was not controlled.

Use dates in ISO order — 2026-07-23 — so files sort chronologically. It is a small thing that makes a folder navigable a year later.

## Record what you did, not just what you got

A short log sheet: date, what changed, why. Three columns and one line per change.

This feels bureaucratic until the first time someone asks why the March figure differs from the version they were sent. Then it is the most valuable sheet in the file.

## Do not suppress errors on the calculation spine

An error is telling you something. Wrapping it in `IFERROR` and returning zero converts a visible problem into an invisible one and produces a confident wrong answer.

Use `IFNA` where a missing value is genuinely expected. Leave everything else visible. A model that errors is a model that is telling you the truth.

## Reconcile to something external

The strongest evidence a workbook can carry is agreement with something outside itself. A bank statement, a trial balance, a system report.

Put the reconciliation in the file, with the difference calculated. A difference cell showing zero is the single most reassuring thing a reviewer can see, and if it is not zero you find out before somebody else does.

## The test

Hand the file to a competent colleague with no explanation and ask them to tell you where one specific number came from.

If they can trace it back to a source without asking you anything, the trail exists. If they cannot, it does not — regardless of how well you understand it yourself.
