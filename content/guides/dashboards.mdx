Most dashboards fail for one of two reasons. Either they show the wrong thing well, or they show the right thing and break the moment someone else touches them.

Both are avoidable, and neither is about chart formatting.

## The structural decision

Build your dashboard in three layers, on separate sheets:

1. **Data** — the raw records, one row each, untouched
2. **Calculations** — the aggregations that feed the charts
3. **Presentation** — the charts and the layout

Charts read from layer 2, never from layer 1.

This sounds like extra work. It is what makes the difference between a dashboard that survives a year and one that breaks the first time the source data changes shape.

A chart built directly on raw transaction data has an implicit dependency on the exact rows and columns being where they were. Add a column, and the chart series points at the wrong field. Nothing errors. The chart just shows something different and looks fine.

A chart built on a small aggregation table has one dependency, in a place you can see.

## Choosing a chart

Most chart choices are decided by what you are comparing:

| What you are showing | Use |
|---|---|
| Change over time | Line |
| Comparison across categories | Bar, horizontal if the labels are long |
| Part of a whole | Stacked bar, or just a table |
| Relationship between two measures | Scatter |
| One number that matters | Large text, not a chart |

That last row is the one people miss. If the message is "revenue was £4.2m", a gauge or a single-value doughnut adds nothing. Large clear type says it better and takes a second to read.

**On pie charts:** they work for two or three slices and become unreadable beyond that, because humans compare angles poorly. A bar chart sorted by size shows the same data more clearly, almost always. Use a pie when the point is genuinely "this one is about half" and never for a ranking.

## Sort your bars

An unsorted bar chart makes the reader do work you could have done for them. Sorting by value turns "which is biggest" from a scanning exercise into an instant answer.

The exception is a natural order — months, or age bands — where reordering would be confusing.

## Start bar charts at zero

A bar chart's message is carried by the relative lengths of the bars. Truncating the axis exaggerates differences, sometimes dramatically. A 3% gap can be made to look like a doubling.

Line charts are different: they show change, and a zero baseline often compresses the shape into a flat line that hides the trend. Truncating a line axis is legitimate, provided the axis is labelled honestly.

## Colour

Pick one accent colour and one neutral. Use the accent for the thing you want noticed and the neutral for everything else.

Dashboards with eight colours are asking the reader to work out what the colours mean. Dashboards with one accent are telling them where to look.

If a category has an established meaning — red for over budget, for instance — keep it consistent across every chart on the page. Nothing undermines a report faster than red meaning "bad" in one chart and "region 3" in the next.

## The aggregation layer

This is where `SUMIFS` earns its place. A calculation sheet is typically a small grid:

```
=SUMIFS(Data!$F:$F, Data!$C:$C, $A5, Data!$B:$B, B$4)
```

Regions down the side, months across the top, one formula filled across and down. Note the dollar signs — locking the column on `$A5` and the row on `B$4` is what lets one formula fill the whole grid.

That grid is what your chart points at. It is small, it is visible, and if a number looks wrong you can see immediately which cell to check.

## What to leave out

**Every metric you have.** A dashboard is a set of decisions about what matters. Twenty charts is a data dump, and the reader will look at none of them.

**Gauges and speedometers.** They consume a great deal of space to show one number against one target. A number with a small variance figure beside it does the same job in a fraction of the space.

**3D anything.** Perspective distorts the very lengths and angles the chart exists to convey.

## Test it by handing it over

The real test of a dashboard is not whether it looks good. It is whether someone else can update it next month without asking you how.

If the answer involves explaining which cells to overwrite and which to leave alone, the structure needs work — not the formatting.
