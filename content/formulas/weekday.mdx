mistakes:
  - level: error
    title: The default is Sunday, not Monday
    body: With no second argument, Sunday returns 1 and Saturday returns 7. Almost every business week starts on Monday, so almost every WEEKDAY formula should pass 2 as the second argument. Omitting it is the most common error in this function.
  - level: warning
    title: Testing for a weekend with the wrong numbers
    body: With return_type 2, the weekend is 6 and 7. With the default, it is 1 and 7. A rule written for one and used with the other misclassifies two days a week and looks fine on a spot check.
  - level: warning
    title: Dates stored as text return #VALUE!
    body: WEEKDAY needs a real date. A cell holding "01/15/2026" as text fails. Dates imported from other systems are text far more often than they look it — a date sitting on the left of its cell rather than the right is the giveaway.
  - level: warning
    title: Assuming it knows about public holidays
    body: WEEKDAY only knows about the seven-day cycle. Christmas Day on a Thursday is a weekday as far as it is concerned. For working days that account for holidays, use NETWORKDAYS with a holiday list.
faq:
  - q: How do I check whether a date is a weekend?
    a: '=IF(WEEKDAY(A2, 2) > 5, "Weekend", "Weekday"). With return_type 2, Saturday is 6 and Sunday is 7, so anything above 5 is the weekend. This reads more clearly than testing the default numbering.'
  - q: What does the return_type argument actually change?
    a: Only which day is numbered 1. Type 1 or omitted starts on Sunday, type 2 starts on Monday, type 3 starts on Monday but counts from 0. The date itself is unaffected — you are choosing a numbering scheme.
  - q: How do I get the day name rather than a number?
    a: Use TEXT — =TEXT(A2,"dddd") returns Monday, and "ddd" returns Mon. WEEKDAY gives you a number to calculate with, TEXT gives you a label to read.
  - q: How do I count how many Mondays are in a range?
    a: '=SUMPRODUCT((WEEKDAY(A2:A100, 2) = 1) * 1). SUMPRODUCT applies WEEKDAY to every date and adds up the TRUEs. Multiplying by 1 converts TRUE and FALSE into 1 and 0.'
  - q: Why does my schedule highlight the wrong days?
    a: Nearly always the missing second argument. A conditional formatting rule written as =WEEKDAY($A2)>5 flags Saturday and Sunday only if you meant the Sunday-first numbering. Add the 2 and the rule matches the week most people work.

## Examples

### 1. The default numbering

<Formula>=WEEKDAY(B2)</Formula>

Row 2 is Monday, January 5, 2026, and this returns 2 — because with no second argument, Sunday is day 1.

### 2. Starting the week on Monday

<Formula>=WEEKDAY(B2, 2)</Formula>

The same date now returns 1. This is the numbering nearly every business actually uses, and passing `2` should be your default habit.

<Callout type="warning" title="The second argument is not optional in practice">
Leaving it out is legal and gives you an American week starting on Sunday. Every downstream test — weekend flags, schedule coloring, "is this day 1 of the week" — then has to be written against that numbering. Pass `2` every time and the numbers line up with how you think about the week.
</Callout>

### 3. Flagging the weekend

<Formula>=IF(WEEKDAY(B4, 2) > 5, "Weekend", "Weekday")</Formula>

Row 4 is Saturday, January 10. With Monday-based numbering, Saturday is 6 and Sunday is 7, so "greater than 5" is the whole test.

Switch the same date to the default numbering and Saturday becomes 7 while Sunday becomes 1 — which is why a weekend test written for one scheme quietly misclassifies days under the other.

### 4. Counting weekend shifts

<Formula>=SUMPRODUCT((WEEKDAY(B2:B7, 2) > 5) * 1)</Formula>

SUMPRODUCT applies WEEKDAY down the whole column and totals the results. The `* 1` converts each TRUE into a 1 so they can be added — a pattern worth recognising, because it turns any row-by-row test into a count.
