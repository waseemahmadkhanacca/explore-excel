mistakes:
  - level: error
    title: Writing a range instead of conditions
    body: '=AND(B2:B7>100) does not test each row separately in older Excel. AND collapses everything to one answer. To test row by row, put a separate AND in each row, or use COUNTIFS to count how many rows satisfy all the conditions.'
  - level: warning
    title: Chaining comparisons the way you would speak
    body: '=AND(1 < B2 < 100) does not mean what it looks like. Excel evaluates it left to right and the result is nonsense. Write two conditions — AND(B2>1, B2<100).'
  - level: warning
    title: Using AND when you mean OR
    body: Every extra condition in an AND makes the result harder to satisfy, never easier. If adding a condition should widen the net rather than narrow it, OR is the function you want.
  - level: warning
    title: Text and blanks are ignored rather than treated as false
    body: A condition that evaluates to text is skipped entirely. If every argument is skipped, AND returns #VALUE!. This matters when conditions reference cells that might be empty.
faq:
  - q: What is the difference between AND and OR?
    a: AND needs every condition to be true. OR needs at least one. They can be nested inside each other — AND(A, OR(B, C)) — to build any rule you need, though beyond two levels it is usually clearer to add a helper column.
  - q: Why does AND return TRUE or FALSE instead of a useful answer?
    a: Because that is its job. AND is a test, not an output. Put it inside IF — =IF(AND(...), "Yes", "No") — to turn the test into something a reader can act on.
  - q: Can I use AND in conditional formatting?
    a: Yes, and it is one of the best uses. Create a rule using a formula, enter =AND($B2<$C2, $D2>10), and only rows meeting every condition are highlighted. Note the dollar signs on the column letters.
  - q: How do I do AND across a whole column at once?
    a: Multiply the conditions rather than using AND — (B2:B7>100)*(C2:C7<60) gives one result per row, which is what FILTER and SUMPRODUCT expect. Multiplication is AND because anything times zero is zero.
  - q: Is there a limit to how many conditions AND takes?
    a: 255. Long before that it becomes unreadable. Past about four conditions, a helper column holding an intermediate test is easier to check and much easier for someone else to follow.

## Examples

### 1. Two tests, one answer

<Formula>=AND(B2>100, C2<60)</Formula>

TRUE, because both are true. Change either condition, or edit the numbers in the grid, and it flips to FALSE the moment one fails.

### 2. Turning the test into a decision

AND on its own tells a reader very little. Wrapped in IF, it does something.

<Formula>=IF(AND(B5<C5, D5>10), "Order now", "Hold")</Formula>

Stock below the reorder point *and* the item costs enough to be worth a purchase order. Either condition alone would be the wrong rule.

<Callout type="tip" title="Where AND really earns its place">
Conditional formatting. Create a new rule, choose "Use a formula", and enter `=AND($B2<$C2, $D2>10)`. Every row meeting both conditions highlights, and it keeps working as the data changes. The dollar signs lock the columns while leaving the row free to move — get those wrong and the highlighting drifts diagonally across the sheet.
</Callout>

### 3. Three conditions

<Formula>=AND(B2>0, B2>C2, D2<100)</Formula>

In stock, above the reorder point, and not an expensive item. Each extra condition narrows the result further — an AND can only ever get harder to satisfy.
